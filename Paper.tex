\begin{abstract}
Separating overlapping particle showers in the CMS High Granularity
Calorimeter (HGCAL) requires reconstruction methods that exploit sparse,
irregular energy deposits. GravNet-based approaches exchange information
between hits through neighbor graphs in learned coordinate spaces, requiring
repeated graph construction. We present \textbf{FastGraph}, a CUDA/PyTorch
implementation of exact $k$-nearest-neighbor search motivated by this
computational task. It uses a principal component analysis (PCA) subspace
for cell-list pruning while evaluating candidate distances in the original
feature space. An orthonormal projection supplies a lower bound for exact
search in exact arithmetic; finite-precision correctness is assessed
separately in bounded tests. The study covers $d{=}2$--$10$, with PCA used
at $d{=}4$--$10$, and includes an opt-in differentiable GravNet integration.
On a 5\,M-point, single-segment HGCAL detector-feature stress test at
$d{=}8$, $k{=}40$, FastGraph constructs the graph in \fgcPCADeight,
with speedups of up to $41\times$ over FAISS-GPU and $19\times$ over cuVS
brute force. The measured speedup over approximate CAGRA is $17\times$,
without a matched-recall comparison. These results establish a kernel-level
advantage on the tested detector-feature distributions; event-batched
learned-coordinate workloads, reconstruction performance, and end-to-end
model throughput remain to be evaluated.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Calorimetric particle reconstruction begins with many local energy
deposits and must infer the particles that produced them. An incident
electron, photon, or hadron can initiate a shower of secondary particles; nearby
showers can overlap, making the assignment of measured deposits to
individual particles ambiguous. This challenge is particularly acute in
the High-Luminosity Large Hadron Collider, where additional proton--proton
collisions in the same bunch crossing (pileup) increase the density of
energy deposits. The CMS High Granularity Calorimeter (HGCAL) is designed to
resolve shower structure through fine transverse and longitudinal
segmentation~\cite{pitters2018hgcal}. Exploiting that information requires
algorithms that separate showers while retaining the information needed
to estimate particle energies and positions. Published HGCAL studies
have therefore explored joint hit clustering, particle identification,
and energy regression within computational and memory
constraints~\cite{qasim2021multiparticle}.

The computational representation matters because detector hits form a
sparse, irregular point set rather than a regular image. A graph neural
network (GNN) represents each hit as a node and uses edges to exchange
information between selected hits. In GravNet~\cite{Qasim2019GravNet},
the network maps hit features to learned coordinates and connects each
hit to a fixed number of nearby points in that coordinate space.
Distance-weighted aggregation then updates the hit representation.
These learned coordinates express similarity relevant to reconstruction;
they need not coincide with physical detector positions, and a neighbor
edge is not itself an assignment to a shower. Together with an object
condensation loss, which trains the network to organize hits into
reconstructable objects, this approach has been investigated for
multi-particle calorimeter reconstruction~\cite{Kieseler2020ObjectCondensation,qasim2021multiparticle,bhattacharya2022gnn}.

Constructing these neighborhoods is a recurring numerical task. For
$N$ hits with $d$ coordinates, exhaustive all-query neighbor search
requires $O(N^2d)$ distance work. Unlike a static detector adjacency,
a learned-coordinate graph must be rebuilt when the coordinates change
between network layers or forward passes. The original GravNet study
uses $d{=}4$ for this search~\cite{Qasim2019GravNet}, motivating the
$d{=}4$--$10$ range examined here. Reducing graph-construction cost
could make such reconstruction models less expensive to execute;
the gain in a complete model also depends on feature transformations,
message passing, and backpropagation. We therefore study neighbor
construction as a computational component and do not assume that its
speedup transfers unchanged to event reconstruction.

A useful starting point for computational physics is the cell list,
familiar from molecular-simulation neighbor searches~\cite{verlet1967,howard2016neighbor}.
Points are assigned to spatial bins so that nearby bins can be searched
without evaluating every pairwise distance. A grid with
$n_{\text{bins}}$ divisions along each of $d$ axes contains
$n_{\text{bins}}^d$ cells, however, making a full grid costly even at
moderate $d$. FastGraph instead bins in a small principal-component
subspace and evaluates candidate distances using all original
coordinates. The projection serves only as a search index: it does not
replace the distance metric chosen by the reconstruction model.
Because an orthonormal projection cannot increase a distance, bounds
in the binning space can certify termination of the full-space search
in exact arithmetic. Section~\ref{sec:exactness} states the assumptions
and distinguishes this argument from FP32 validation.

The contribution is the formulation and CUDA/PyTorch realization of
this sampled-PCA cell-list search for repeated, low-to-moderate-dimensional
graph construction. PCA indexing and spatial pruning are established
ideas; our study examines their combination with full-space distance
evaluation, event-local search support, and distance gradients in a
GPU graph-construction primitive. Exact search provides a specified
distance criterion without an approximate index's recall parameter,
although tied distances can admit several valid edge sets. We do not
establish that exact neighbors improve HGCAL physics performance over
approximate neighbors.

We evaluate the implementation on detector-derived HGCAL hit features
and on controlled synthetic distributions. The HGCAL benchmark
concatenates events into one segment and uses stored feature columns,
whose semantics and preprocessing are unavailable in the supplied
artifact; it is a kernel stress test rather than a sample of learned
coordinates from a reconstruction model. At $d{=}8$, $N{=}5$\,M,
$k{=}40$, FastGraph takes \fgcPCADeight\ versus \faissDeight\ for
exact FAISS-GPU and \cuvsBFDeight\ for exact cuVS brute force.
Across $d{=}2$--$10$ at this $(N,k)$ point, geometric-mean speedups
are \geoFaiss\ and \geoCuvs, respectively. The $d{=}2$--$3$
measurements are axis-aligned controls; PCA is used at $d{=}4$--$10$.
The synthetic study and the direct three-dimensional comparison
identify distributions on which the advantage decreases or other
methods win. These tests establish where the computational component
is effective; event-preserving learned-coordinate timings and
reconstruction observables remain necessary to assess its benefit
to the motivating physics application.

The following sections place the method among existing neighbor-search
approaches, derive its search criterion, and describe its implementation.
The performance study then separates detector-feature timings,
distribution and parameter controls, memory measurements, and correctness
checks before discussing the limits of the evidence.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related-work}

Neighbor search in a reconstruction model can be accelerated either by
evaluating all distances efficiently or by reducing the candidate set.
The first approach maps naturally to GPUs. FAISS~\cite{johnson2019billion} provides a
highly tuned \texttt{IndexFlatL2} exact brute-force GPU index together
with the fast $k$-selection primitives that downstream methods reuse.
RAPIDS \texttt{cuVS} exposes a comparable exact brute-force GPU
implementation that we use as the strongest exact baseline in this
work; KeOps~\cite{feydy2020fast} provides symbolic matrix reductions
with automatic differentiation that can express exact $k$NN without
materializing the full distance matrix. Direct exact GPU $k$NN-graph
construction has also been studied in the multi-GPU and cluster
settings~\cite{dashti2013efficient,komarov2014fast}. PyTorch Geometric
exposes the widely used \texttt{knn\_graph} interface through its
\texttt{torch\_cluster} extension~\cite{fey2019pyg}; we treat it as a
framework interface rather than a distinct algorithmic baseline.
FastGraph is not
the first exact GPU $k$NN library; it specializes the accelerator for
the small-$d$ exact graph-construction setting that arises in dynamic
GNN layers.

Reducing candidate work instead requires a spatial index. Cell lists
are widely used in molecular simulations, including GPU neighbor-list
construction~\cite{verlet1967,howard2016neighbor}. For nearest-neighbor
search, points can be sorted by bin and searched through expanding
shells until the current neighbor radius excludes all unvisited cells.
Their grid storage grows exponentially with the number of binning
axes, which motivates indexing only a few coordinates.
Qasim's PhD thesis~\cite{qasim2023thesis} discusses the practical grid
dimensionality limit in the GNN context. FastGraph uses a principal subspace
for the grid coordinates; an axis-aligned first-column choice is retained only
as an internal ablation.

Choosing those coordinates adaptively connects cell lists to a broader
history of projected indexing. Using a principal-component projection as a coordinate system for
spatial indexing has a long history; Sproull's $k$-d tree
refinements~\cite{sproull1991refinements} already noted that
splitting on the direction of maximum variance yields more balanced
partitions than splitting on a coordinate axis. Principal-axis tree
variants use a similar idea for CPU-side neighbor search. Neither PCA
indexing nor lower-bound pruning is new by itself. FastGraph applies a
partial top-$k$ projection to a uniform GPU cell list, retains original
$d$-dimensional distances, and exposes the resulting search to dynamic
graph layers. The contraction $V_k V_k^\top \preceq I_d$ supplies its
exact-arithmetic termination criterion (Section~\ref{sec:method}); the
experiments assess the benefit of this choice of binning basis.

Other spatial indices provide particularly strong alternatives in
three dimensions. A separate line of work targets exact GPU $k$NN through dedicated
spatial-acceleration structures that exploit the GPU's
ray-tracing hardware or BVH machinery. RTNN~\cite{wald2019rtnn},
RT-kNNS Unbound~\cite{evangelou2021rtknn}, and
Arkade~\cite{nagarajan2023arkade} retarget the OptiX BVH unit for
neighbor search and report large speedups over shader-core baselines
in 3D. LBVH-kNN~\cite{jakob2021optimized} (\texttt{cupy-knn}) attains
similar gains via a left-balanced LBVH built without dedicated
RT hardware. CLOVER~\cite{kamel2025clover} introduces a GPU-native,
Voronoi spatio-graph approach to exact $k$NN and reports
$4\times$ over an ``optimized grid'' baseline and
$230\times$ over FAISS on low-dimensional spatial data. All of these methods are
implemented and evaluated for two- or three-dimensional inputs: Arkade
notes that RT cores build BVHs only on three-dimensional data, CLOVER's
reference implementation asserts \texttt{D == 3}, and LBVH-kNN is
evaluated exclusively in 3D. We include a direct head-to-head against
CLOVER at $d{=}3$ in Section~\ref{sec:clover}, but the released methods
do not provide a $d{=}4$--$10$ path comparable to the one evaluated here.

When exact neighbors are unnecessary, GPU-resident approximate nearest
neighbor (ANN) systems offer a further option. SONG~\cite{zhao2020song} decomposes graph
traversal into GPU-friendly stages; GGNN~\cite{ggnn} builds a
hierarchical $k$NN graph for index construction and search;
CAGRA~\cite{ootomo2023cagra}, distributed as part of RAPIDS
\texttt{cuVS}, builds a high-quality graph index with an IVF-PQ or
NN-Descent initialization. ANN-Descent variants have also been adapted
to reduce memory traffic during construction~\cite{wang2021large}.
These systems expose a tunable recall--throughput tradeoff for
high-throughput vector retrieval; FastGraph instead targets exact,
small-$d$ graph construction. We benchmark CAGRA and GGNN as approximate
comparators (Section~\ref{sec:recall}) but do not include CPU-only ANN
systems (HNSW, ScaNN, Annoy)~\cite{malkov2020hnsw,guo2020scann,bernhardsson2013annoy}
in the timing tables: the point tensors and large intermediate arrays remain
GPU-resident, and a CPU-side detour for bulk data would not be a fair comparator.

The relevance of these search methods to physics depends on how the
graph enters the reconstruction algorithm. Beyond GravNet and object
condensation, dynamic neighbor graphs also appear in particle
classification and tracking. DGCNN/EdgeConv recomputes the
graph in feature space at each layer~\cite{wang2019dynamic};
ParticleNet adapts the same idea to jet
tagging~\cite{qu2020particlenet}; GravNet/GarNet
layers~\cite{Qasim2019GravNet} and the object condensation
loss~\cite{Kieseler2020ObjectCondensation} have been studied for HGCAL
reconstruction~\cite{qasim2021multiparticle,bhattacharya2022gnn}; the
HEP.TrkX/Exa.TrkX line of work uses learned-embedding graphs for
tracking~\cite{ju2021performance,lazar2022accelerating,choma2020track}.
FastGraph supplies a computational primitive for the neighbor-search
step in such models. This study concentrates on the low-dimensional
search regime motivated by calorimetry; it does not evaluate the wider
feature spaces that can occur in later EdgeConv or ParticleNet layers.

\section{The PCA-Subspace Binned $k$NN Algorithm}
\label{sec:method}

In the algorithm and complexity analysis, $K$ denotes the requested
neighborhood size (including self), and $k$ denotes the binning-subspace
dimension. Experimental figure labels use the conventional $k$ for
neighborhood size; binning dimensions are identified explicitly.

\subsection{Phase 1: Subspace estimation}
\label{sec:phase1}

Given the input coordinates
$X \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times d}$ for the current call, FastGraph samples
up to 50,000 rows, centers that sample, and computes the top-$k$ principal
axes with \texttt{torch.pca\_lowrank}. The current release fits one
projection for the complete coordinate tensor rather than a separate
projection for each \texttt{row\_splits} segment. The number of
binning axes $k$ is a user-facing hyperparameter; we default to
$k = \mbdPCAdefault$ for the HGCAL workloads in this paper, justified
empirically in the ablation of Section~\ref{sec:mbd-ablation}. The
resulting projection matrix $V_k \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times k}$
satisfies $V_k^\top V_k = I_k$ and $V_k V_k^\top \preceq I_d$. The
projected coordinates used for binning are $Y = X V_k$. PCA estimation
is performed once per call on the GPU and is included in the reported
FastGraph timing. The random subsample follows PyTorch's active random
number-generator state.

This construction simplifies when the input dimensionality $d$ does not exceed the binning
dimensionality $k$, FastGraph skips PCA estimation entirely and
binds the binning axes to the raw input axes; the projection
$V_k$ reduces to the identity (up to permutation), $Y = X$, and the
algorithm behaves exactly as a classical axis-aligned cell list. With
the default $k = \mbdPCAdefault$, the $d \in \{2, 3\}$ gains therefore
come from the underlying cell-list machinery; the PCA contribution
begins at $d > k$, covering the $d{=}4$--$10$ detector-feature
experiments motivated by the GravNet search dimension.

\subsection{Phase 2: Binning in the projected subspace}
\label{sec:phase2}

We partition $Y$ with a uniform grid whose scalar width $w$ is shared
across the $k$ projected axes. The division count is set heuristically
to balance bin density against neighborhood reach,
\[
  n_{\text{bins}} = \left(\frac{32\,n_{\text{elems}}}{K}\right)^{\frac{1}{k}},
\]
where the released implementation uses the historical occupancy proxy
\[
  n_{\text{elems}}=\lfloor\max(\texttt{row\_splits})/
  |\texttt{row\_splits}|\rfloor+1,
\]
and $K$ is the requested neighborhood size. The integer division count
is clamped to $[5,30]$. For the one-segment
benchmark input \texttt{row\_splits}=$[0,N]$, this proxy is approximately
$N/2$; it is a performance heuristic and does not enter the exactness
condition. Points are sorted by bin index, and cumulative offsets are
stored so that each bin can be addressed as a contiguous slice of the
sorted point array. Row splits, which delineate the per-event
boundaries inside a batched tensor, are honored by the binning kernel
so that no query bin spans events.

\subsection{Phase 3: Per-query search with full-dimensional distances}
\label{sec:phase3}

For each query $x_q$, the search starts in the bin containing
$y_q = x_q V_k$ and expands through successive shells of $k$-D
hypercube bins. For every candidate $u$ in a visited bin the
\emph{full-dimensional} squared Euclidean distance
$\|x_q - x_u\|^2 = \sum_{i=1}^{d}(x_{q,i} - x_{u,i})^2$ is computed
(not the $k$-dimensional projected distance). The current best
neighbor radius $r_K$ and a fixed-size top-$K$ candidate buffer are
maintained incrementally.

\subsection{Why this is exact: the contraction argument}
\label{sec:exactness}

For finite inputs with valid row splits, a supported projection dimension
$2 \le k \le d$, and at least $K$ eligible points in each segment (or in
its direction-masked subset), consider the standard cube-radius termination
test applied to bins in the projected subspace while all candidate distances
are evaluated in the full input space. The
exactness of this test rests on a single observation about the partial
projection. For any two points $x_a, x_b \in \mathbb{R}^d$,
\[
  \|y_a - y_b\|^2
  = (x_a - x_b)^\top V_k V_k^\top (x_a - x_b)
  \le \|x_a - x_b\|^2,
\]
because $V_k V_k^\top$ has eigenvalues in $\{0,1\}$ and is therefore
$\preceq I_d$. Equivalently, the projection is contractive: the
projected distance never overestimates the true distance.

After visiting all bins within the current hypercube shell of radius
$s$, any unvisited bin lies at projected distance at least
$w \cdot s$ from $y_q$, where $w$ is the scalar width shared by all
projected axes in the released implementation. Thus any unvisited point
satisfies $\|y_q - y_u\|^2 \ge (w \cdot s)^2$. Combined with the
contraction above, $\|x_q - x_u\|^2 \ge (w \cdot s)^2$, so
once the top-$K$ buffer has filled and $(w \cdot s)^2 > r_K^2$, no unseen
point can be closer in the true distance than the current $K$-th
neighbor. The algorithm may safely terminate; the returned $K$
neighbors are exact in exact arithmetic. This argument assumes an
orthonormal projection and does not bound floating-point errors in the
projection or pruning test. The FP32 implementation is validated separately
against brute force at tractable scale, with the distance criteria and
numerical tolerances reported in Section~\ref{sec:recall}.

\subsection{Search implementation}

The \texttt{binstepper} is the small bookkeeping utility that
enumerates the surface of the $s$-th hypercube shell around the
query bin. It linearises each shell into a flat sequence, converts
each step back into per-dimension offsets, discards interior cells,
and yields the next surface bin's flat index. Exhaustive enumeration
of the required shell cells is necessary for the termination argument;
the utility separates this bookkeeping from candidate-distance
evaluation in Algorithm~\ref{alg:knn}. Keeping the shell enumerator
separate from the distance kernel also makes the projected walk
event-local: the same row-split offsets and contiguous bin slices can
be reused across all queries in the batch.

Algorithm~\ref{alg:knn} makes the projected-vs-original-space
distinction explicit: the bin indices (\texttt{binIdx}) and the
stepper operate on $y$-space; the distance function
\texttt{calcDist} operates on the original $x$-space coordinates;
the termination test references the projected-space cube radius;
the contraction argument certifies the result. This separation keeps
the implementation and the proof aligned: projected-space search only
decides which bins to visit, while exactness is enforced by the
original-space distance computation.

\begin{algorithm}[!htbp]
\small
\DontPrintSemicolon
\KwIn{\texttt{coords}[$n_v$][$n_c$] (original-space $X$), $V_k$, \texttt{binIdx}[$n_v$] (projected-space bins), \texttt{binOffsets}[$n_v$][$n_b{+}1$], \texttt{binBounds}[$n_B$], \texttt{binCounts}[$n_b$], scalar \texttt{binWidth}, $n_v,K,n_c,n_b,n_B$}
\KwOut{\texttt{neighbors}[$n_v$][$K$] indices including slot 0 self by convention, $r_K^2$ values}
\ForEach{$v \in [0,n_v)$ \textbf{in parallel}}{
  \texttt{neighbors}[v][0]$\leftarrow(v,0)$; \texttt{filled}$\leftarrow1$; $(\texttt{maxIdx},\texttt{maxD2})\leftarrow(0,0)$\;
  $\texttt{totalBins}\leftarrow\prod_{d=0}^{n_b-1}\texttt{binCounts}[d]$, $\texttt{gOff}\leftarrow \texttt{totalBins}\cdot\lfloor \texttt{binIdx}[v]/\texttt{totalBins}\rfloor$\;
  \texttt{step}$\leftarrow$\texttt{BinStepper}(\texttt{binCounts}, \texttt{binOffsets}[v]$[1..n_b]$) \tcp*{operates in projected $y$-space}
  $w\leftarrow \texttt{binWidth}$\;
  \texttt{cont}$\leftarrow$ true; $s\leftarrow0$ \tcp*{$s$: current cube-shell radius (projected space)}
  \While{\texttt{cont}}{
    \texttt{step.setDistance}($s$); \texttt{cont}$\leftarrow$ false\;
    \While{$(\mathtt{off} \leftarrow \mathtt{step.step()}) \neq -1$}{
      $\texttt{idx}\leftarrow \texttt{off}+\texttt{gOff}$\;
      \If{$\texttt{idx}<0$ \textbf{or} $\texttt{idx}\ge n_B-1$}{\Continue}
      $(s_b,e_b)\leftarrow(\texttt{binBounds}[\texttt{idx}],\texttt{binBounds}[\texttt{idx}{+}1])$\;
      \If{$s_b=e_b$}{\texttt{cont}$\leftarrow$ true; \Continue}
      \For{$u\in[s_b,e_b)$}{
        \If{$u{=}v$}{\Continue}
        $\texttt{d2}\leftarrow \texttt{calcDist}(v,u)$ \tcp*{full $d$-dim $\sum_i(x_{v,i}-x_{u,i})^2$, NOT projected}
        \If{$\texttt{filled}<K$}{\texttt{neighbors}[v][\texttt{filled}]$\leftarrow(u,\texttt{d2})$;\ \If{$\texttt{d2}>\texttt{maxD2}$}{$(\texttt{maxIdx},\texttt{maxD2})\leftarrow(\texttt{filled},\texttt{d2})$}; \texttt{filled}++}
        \ElseIf{$\texttt{d2}<\texttt{maxD2}$}{\texttt{neighbors}[v][\texttt{maxIdx}]$\leftarrow(u,\texttt{d2})$;\ $(\texttt{maxIdx},\texttt{maxD2})\leftarrow\texttt{findMaxDist}(\texttt{neighbors}[v])$}
      }
      \texttt{cont}$\leftarrow$ true
    }
    \If{$\texttt{filled}{=}K$ \textbf{and} $(w\cdot s)^2>\texttt{maxD2}$}{\Break}
    $s$++
  }
}
\caption{\textbf{PCA-Subspace Binned-Select-kNN}: bins live in projected $y$-space; distances are computed in original $x$-space. Slot 0 is the query point itself, matching the FastGraph tensor convention; downstream message-passing code may drop or ignore this self edge.}
\label{alg:knn}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Complexity}

For a single segment, let $B = n_{\text{bins}}^{k}$ be the size of the
bin grid; for multiple segments, let $B$ denote their total allocated bin
count. Both PCA-subspace binning and the axis-aligned $k$-coordinate
control avoid a full $n_{\text{bins}}^{d}$ grid. PCA changes the choice of
binning axes rather than the grid's dimensional dependence.

To quantify setup work, let $S=\min(N,50{,}000)$ be the PCA sample
size and $q=\min(k+2,d)$ the low-rank solve size. For the fixed number
of randomized iterations used by \texttt{torch.pca\_lowrank}, the
subspace fit costs $O(Sdq+(S+d)q^2)$, followed by $O(Ndk)$ to project
all points. This is a sampled low-rank computation, not a full
$O(Nd^2+d^3)$ eigendecomposition. Binning, sorting, and offset
construction take $O(N \log N + B)$.

The associated auxiliary memory is $O(N(d + k + K) + B)$:
sorted coordinates, projected coordinates, permutation, neighbor
indices, and bin offsets.

Search work depends on the input distribution. Let $C_v$ be the number of candidate
points evaluated for query $v$, and $E_v$ the number of cell-offset tests
performed by the shell enumerator, including rejected offsets. Each
candidate requires $O(d)$ distance work and at most $O(K)$ for the
top-$K$ update; cell-offset bookkeeping costs $O(k)$ per test. The query
cost is therefore $O(kE_v+C_v(d+K))$. Uniform projected density alone
does not imply $C_v=O(K)$: many points may be close in projection but far
apart in the discarded dimensions. When pruning and bin occupancy keep
both $C_v$ and $E_v$ proportional to $K$, this reduces to $O(K(d+K))$
for fixed $k$. In the worst case, every point in a segment is evaluated,
giving $O(N_v(d+K))$ candidate work for segment size $N_v$, in addition
to shell enumeration.

Combining setup with the work summed across queries gives
\[
 O\!\left(Sdq+(S+d)q^2+Ndk+N\log N+B+
 \sum_{v=1}^{N}\bigl[kE_v+C_v(d+K)\bigr]\right).
\]
Queries execute in parallel on the GPU; elapsed time also depends on
load imbalance, memory traffic, and resource occupancy.

\subsection{Gradient flow and PyTorch integration}

FastGraph exposes gradients through the continuous outputs of the
kernel---the selected squared distances and the input coordinates---
while treating the discrete neighbor indices and the projection
matrix $V_k$ as constants in the backward pass. Concretely, the
forward returns $(\text{idx}, \text{dist}^2)$ and the backward
applies the chain rule with respect to $\text{dist}^2$ and the input
coordinates, yielding a subgradient of the piecewise-smooth loss
induced by the hard neighbor selection. This is the same gradient
model used by any fixed-index distance layer
(e.g.,\ \texttt{torch.cdist} followed by \texttt{topk}) and integrates
naturally into existing GNN autograd graphs. Treating $V_k$ as fixed per forward--backward pair keeps graph selection
consistent within that pair; the projection is refreshed at the next forward.
The CUDA backward uses atomic accumulation and is therefore not guaranteed
to be bitwise deterministic.

The PCA entry point is an eager-mode Python wrapper around the scripted
axis-aligned primitive and the custom CUDA operation. A minimal usage
example is:

\begin{lstlisting}[language=Python, float=htbp, caption={Minimal eager-mode PCA-FastGraph usage.}]
from fastgraphcompute import binned_select_knn_pca
from fastgraphcompute.gnn_ops import GravNetOp

# Direct call (eager mode)
idx, dist2 = binned_select_knn_pca(K=40, coords=x, row_splits=rs,
                                   max_bin_dims=3)
# idx:   (N, K) int64 neighbor indices (constant in backward)
# dist2: (N, K) float32 squared distances (differentiable)
loss = model(dist2).sum()
loss.backward()  # gradients flow through dist2 to x

# Opt-in PCA neighbor construction inside GravNetOp (eager mode)
layer = GravNetOp(in_channels=16, out_channels=32,
                  space_dimensions=8, propagate_dimensions=32,
                  k=40, use_pca=True, max_bin_dims=3)
out, idx, dist2, space = layer(features, rs)
\end{lstlisting}

\section{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:expsetup}

The experiments ask whether the proposed search reduces graph-construction
cost on detector-derived inputs, how that behavior depends on the input
distribution and binning dimension, and what correctness evidence supports
the implementation. All GPU experiments use a single NVIDIA A100 80\,GB PCIe with CUDA
12.1 and PyTorch 2.5. Compared backends are exact FAISS-GPU
\texttt{IndexFlatL2}~\cite{johnson2019billion}, cuVS brute force
(also exact), CAGRA via cuVS~\cite{ootomo2023cagra}
(approximate), and GGNN~\cite{ggnn} (approximate); FastGraph is
compiled from the released source identified in the availability statement.

The detector-derived benchmark matrix comprises \hgcalRecHits\ CMS HGCAL
simulation recHits concatenated across events, with ten stored feature
columns per hit. A recHit is a reconstructed calorimeter energy deposit;
the benchmark uses the stored numerical vectors without inferring their
physical column definitions. The harness deterministically takes the first
$N$ rows and first $d$ columns; the available artifact does not identify
the column names, units, preprocessing, or normalization. Increasing $d$
therefore changes both the number and identity of included features,
and PCA remains sensitive to their numerical scales. Each timed slice
is passed to the implementations as one segment rather than preserving
event boundaries. This construction is a large-$N$ stress test of the
kernels on detector-derived features; it is not an event-batched GNN
workload or a learned-coordinate sample. Synthetic comparisons use
isotropic Gaussian $\mathcal{N}(0,I_d)$ samples, allowing dimensionality
to be varied without the changing feature identities and scales of
the detector-derived matrix.

To compare computational cost on these inputs, all HGCAL timing plots
and the headline table use the completed
matched-input, serialized \texttt{--gres=mps:100} campaign only; older
\texttt{mps:50} and exclusive-allocation rows are excluded. Every backend
has the same input protocol. FastGraph/PCA, FAISS, cuVS BF, and GGNN
have seven repetitions per completed plotted cell; CAGRA-NN-Descent has
three. Inputs are copied to a
Torch CUDA tensor before timing: FastGraph and GGNN consume that tensor
directly, FAISS uses its Torch bridge, and cuVS BF and CAGRA use a
pointer-identical CuPy DLPack view created before the timer. The timed
region therefore includes index construction and search, but excludes data
loading and host-to-device transfer for every backend. All timed regions are
bracketed by device synchronization. Figures and scalar comparisons report
medians across successful repetitions; error bars show the interquartile
range.

One backend requires a different index-construction path for these inputs.
The default CAGRA IVF-PQ build path fails in the 5\,M-point timing campaign at $d \ge 5$
with a RAFT assertion about invalid or duplicated neighbor nodes. We did
not isolate the internal cause of this failure. We therefore use CAGRA's
\texttt{build\_algo="nn\_descent"}
path for the headline timing campaign; it avoids the PQ initialization,
succeeds across the full $d{=}2$--$10$ sweep, and is the robust full-sweep
CAGRA timing baseline. The archived recall diagnostic predates that campaign
and is labeled separately as default-build CAGRA.

For FastGraph, the binning subspace size $k$ is exposed as
\texttt{max\_bin\_dims}. The benchmark binary and evaluated kernel surface
provide compile-time template instantiations for $k \in \{2,3,4,5\}$; we
use $k=\mbdPCAdefault$ as the default for HGCAL and ablate that surface in
Section~\ref{sec:mbd-ablation}. The immutable source release also contains
experimental $k=6,7$ dispatches used to probe the design boundary, but they
are not part of the recommended headline configuration; $k=7$ exceeds the
current per-block register budget for some configurations. The empirical
optimum on HGCAL data is well inside the evaluated range.

The result splits along the structure of the input distribution.

\subsection{Recall evaluation}
\label{sec:recall}

We use an archived broad recall sweep against a brute-force reference on
HGCAL data up to $N{=}5\times 10^5$. This diagnostic invokes the
axis-aligned \texttt{binned\_select\_knn} control rather than the PCA wrapper.
Its CAGRA rows use the default graph build and \texttt{itopk\_size}=512;
its GGNN rows use $\tau_{\text{query}}=0.8$. This archived experiment is
separate from the headline timing campaign, which uses CAGRA--NN-Descent.
We use these archived outputs only to report recall; none of their timing
values enters the performance comparisons.

Because multiple recHits can have tied distances in the selected feature
space, neighbor indices are not unique.
We use \emph{distance-based recall}, counting a predicted neighbor as correct
when its squared distance $\widehat{D}_{v,j}$ satisfies
\[
 \widehat{D}_{v,j} \le (1+10^{-4})D_{v,(K)}+10^{-10},
\]
where $D_{v,(K)}$ is the $K$-th brute-force squared distance, including
self. Recall is the fraction of the $NK$ returned entries satisfying
this threshold. FastGraph and its reference use element-wise squared
$\ell_2$ distances, which avoid the cancellation that can affect coincident-hit
cases under the BLAS expansion. FAISS-GPU \texttt{IndexFlatL2} is exact under
its own L2 kernel and is shown as such. Exactness here means satisfying the
fixed distance criterion, not producing a unique graph topology when ties are
present.

At the common representative cell, the FastGraph axis control attains recall
$=1.0$, while neither approximate backend reaches exact recall at its recorded
highest-quality setting. Table~\ref{tab:recall} reports means over the three
repetitions of that cell. We do not report a cross-grid mean because the
archived default-build CAGRA data cover $d=2$--$7$, whereas the GGNN and
FastGraph data extend through $d=10$.

\subsection{Connection to calorimeter reconstruction}
\label{sec:physics-connection}

GravNet-style layers~\cite{Qasim2019GravNet} construct a fresh kNN
graph in a learned latent space at every forward pass, so the
kNN substrate is on the gradient path of every training step.
FastGraph provides an implementation path into this setting through \texttt{GravNetOp}, a
PyTorch module that wraps coordinate transformation, kNN graph
construction (via the opt-in \texttt{use\_pca=True} PCA-subspace path of
Section~\ref{sec:method}), and message passing into a single
autograd-compatible operation. Gradients flow through the selected
distances and the input coordinates; the discrete neighbor indices
and the per-call projection $V_k$ are held fixed across each
forward--backward pair so that graph selection is consistent within the
pair; atomic gradient accumulation is not guaranteed to be bitwise
deterministic.

Within the reconstruction chain described in the Introduction, this
operation supplies neighborhoods for hit-feature aggregation. The later
assignment of hits to showers and the inference of particle properties
remain tasks of the trained network and its reconstruction procedure.
FastGraph changes how the specified neighborhoods are found, while
retaining full-coordinate distance evaluation; it does not define a new
shower-clustering objective. In particular, the million-point timing
inputs are not individual collision events, and the separate segmented
correctness tests are not event-batched performance measurements.
The present evidence supports the cost of the search component on the
reported inputs and its software integration, without demonstrating a
change in shower separation, energy response, or event throughput.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

The detector-feature results establish a useful operating regime, but
their interpretation depends on both the indexing geometry and the
relationship of the benchmark to reconstruction. Both FastGraph and
its axis-aligned ablation bound the grid at
$n_{\text{bins}}^{k}$ for a small binning dimension $k$, independent of input
dimension $d$. FastGraph uses a data-adaptive orthonormal basis rather than
tying the grid to a fixed set of input columns. In exact arithmetic this
avoids privileging the first input columns as binning axes; the sampled
finite-precision fit need not be bitwise invariant under column
permutations. PCA is not
invariant to rescaling individual features: their units and preprocessing
change the covariance and therefore the selected subspace. The method
still requires a small $k$ to keep the grid tractable.

For a given metric, pruning is most useful when projected separations
provide informative bounds on full-space distances. Its effectiveness
therefore depends on how much variation the leading components capture.
On the detector-feature matrix used here, FastGraph's
PCA-subspace binning is substantially
faster than axis-aligned cell lists (Section~\ref{sec:mbd-ablation},
geomean speedups in Section~\ref{sec:headline}). On isotropic data, the
leading subspace is an arbitrary orthonormal $k$-dimensional projection and FastGraph
has no preferred principal directions to exploit; in that regime
FastGraph outperforms dense GPU brute force
only while the cell-list pruning advantage outweighs the constant
factor of an optimized BLAS \texttt{gemm}. Section~\ref{sec:synthetic}
documents this on Gaussian $\mathcal{N}(0, I_d)$ inputs and is the
reason the timing claim is restricted to the measured detector-feature
configurations rather than generalized to all HGCAL workloads. Whether trained
learned-latent embeddings exhibit similarly favorable spectra remains to be
tested.

Setup costs also affect transfer to realistic events. PCA estimation
adds a sampled low-rank fit and an $O(Ndk)$ projection, both included
in the measured FastGraph timings. Their relative cost can increase
for smaller segments; the crossover at which an axis-aligned or
brute-force search becomes preferable requires measurement on those
workloads. The current implementation targets a single
GPU; multi-GPU or distributed kNN graph construction is not
supported. The discrete neighbor indices are not differentiable;
FastGraph's autograd contract is over the continuous distances and
coordinates only, matching every other hard-selection kNN layer.
Approximate methods can be faster in some cells: GGNN
wins on raw wall-clock for $d{=}2$--$6$, whereas FastGraph is faster than
GGNN for $d{=}7$--$10$ and faster than CAGRA throughout the headline sweep.
Recall at these timed operating points remains unmeasured.
The appropriate backend therefore depends on dimension, required graph
quality, and end-to-end cost.

The detector-derived timing study is a kernel stress test rather than a
deployment benchmark: it concatenates recHits and passes each slice as a
single segment. Event-preserving row splits can change both the problem
size and the spatial density seen by a cell list. Likewise, the benchmark
features are detector-derived inputs, not embeddings sampled from a
trained model. Event-batched timings and learned-latent coordinates are
therefore required before extrapolating the reported speedups to
end-to-end training throughput. We defer those model-level measurements
to future work and make no end-to-end throughput claim here. In particular,
reducing search time alone does not establish improved energy resolution,
particle-identification performance, or shower separation. Extending
the evaluated input dimension
beyond $d{=}10$ is secondary to those two validation steps;
higher-dimensional feature-space graph layers remain outside the present
claim. A projected-space LBVH is another possible extension when uniform
bins cease to prune effectively.

Choosing between exact and approximate neighborhoods consequently
requires validation in the reconstruction model. General graph-learning studies
show that changes in connectivity can affect predictions~\cite{zugner2018adversarial,klicpera2019diffusion},
but they do not establish the physics impact of approximate neighbor
search in HGCAL reconstruction. Exact search avoids a recall-tuning
parameter; it does not by itself guarantee a unique graph under ties,
bitwise reproducible gradients, or controlled physics systematics.
Moreover, the exact-arithmetic pruning argument does not bound FP32
roundoff, and the PCA-path correctness tests stop at $N=8{,}000$.
Large-scale detector-feature boundary cases and matched-recall timing
curves remain unmeasured.

Backend compatibility is another restriction on the comparison. The
default CAGRA IVF-PQ build path fails in the 5\,M-point timing campaign at $d \ge 5$
(Section~\ref{sec:expsetup}); the NN-Descent fallback succeeds
across the full dimensional sweep but is \spdHGCALcagradeight\
slower than FastGraph at the $d{=}8$ reference cell. We use the
NN-Descent numbers for the headline timing campaign; the archived recall
diagnostic is explicitly labeled as default-build CAGRA. This is an observed compatibility issue
for the tested dataset and software configuration, not evidence of a
generic CAGRA limitation or a diagnosis of the internal failure cause.

\section{Conclusion}

Separating overlapping showers in a high-granularity calorimeter
motivates graph-based reconstruction methods that repeatedly find
neighbors in learned coordinate spaces. FastGraph addresses this
computational step with a PCA-subspace cell list and full-coordinate
distance evaluation. The contractive projection supplies an
exact-arithmetic search criterion, while the released CUDA/PyTorch
implementation provides distance gradients and an opt-in GravNet path.

On the detector-derived recHit stress test at single-segment $N{=}5$\,M,
$k{=}40$, FastGraph is faster than the tested dimension-general exact
GPU baselines across $d{=}2$--$10$. At $d{=}8$, construction takes
\fgcPCADeight, with speedups up to $41\times$ over FAISS-GPU and
$19\times$ over cuVS brute force. The $17\times$ advantage over
approximate CAGRA-NN-Descent is a timing comparison without matched
recall. Comparing PCA and axis-aligned binning at their respective
optima gives a \fgcVanillaBestPCAfactor\ ratio at this reference
cell. These gains depend on the input distribution: isotropic
synthetic inputs become unfavorable as dimension grows, and CLOVER
is about $100\times$ faster at the largest $d{=}3$ HGCAL comparison
point, where FastGraph bypasses PCA.

The demonstrated advance is reduced search cost on the stated
detector-feature workloads. Their unknown feature semantics and
single-segment construction prevent a direct inference about
event-local learned-coordinate graphs. Measuring that workload,
validating its numerical behavior at scale, and profiling complete
reconstruction models are the next steps toward establishing the
method's physics benefit. No improvement in reconstruction accuracy
or end-to-end throughput is claimed by the present measurements.
